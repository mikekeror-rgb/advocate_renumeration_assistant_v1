Advocate Remuneration Assistant

What it does

The corpus is the Advocates Remuneration Order and a set of court rulings, held as 3,414 chunks. A question passes through five steps, and the model never does arithmetic.

  1. Hybrid retrieval. Dense vector search and BM25 keyword search, merged by reciprocal rank fusion.
  2. Case scoping. When a question names a case, retrieval is limited to that case.
  3. Context building. Retrieved chunks go to the model as long context snippets.
  4. Fee calculator. A router spots fee questions and computes the figure in code from the Order's schedule.
  5. Generation. The model answers and cites the chunks it used. The model backend is switched by environment variable.

Why the test can be trusted

An earlier 62-question study had been used while the pipeline was tuned, so it flattered the pipeline. The new test was built afterwards.

  • The pipeline was frozen first (tag pipeline-frozen-v1), then 276 candidate questions were drafted and every one was reviewed by hand: 194 kept, 42 rewritten, 40 dropped.
  • 14 rulings never used in training or earlier evaluation form an unseen stratum of 69 questions. The other strata are seen rulings (48), statute (23), no-answer (56) and fee calculation (40).
  • Gold answers for the 40 fee calculations were computed by hand and compared with the calculator afterwards. All 40 agreed.
  • Three different models played three roles: gpt-oss-20b wrote the training data, qwen/qwen3.8-27b wrote the questions and gpt-5.4-mini judges the answers.
  • The hypotheses, the analysis plan and the set's hash (95ddd72d65bffeca) were committed and tagged before any system under test was run.

Where the errors come from

On the 117 ruling questions, the gold evidence was among the retrieved chunks 98.3% of the time for the base system (115 of 117). Retrieval is not the bottleneck. Cutting snippets to 1,000 characters left that figure unchanged at 98.3%, yet cost 26.1 points on unseen rulings. The evidence was retrieved but, as far as these data show, the shortened snippet no longer carried the answer.

What each component costs in latency

The components that protect accuracy add almost no time. Switching the calculator off made answers slower, not faster, so the deterministic path is both more accurate and cheaper.

Shorter snippets save about 0.3 seconds on the mean and cost 11.9 points of accuracy, so that trade is not worth making. The calculator switch also turns off the guard, whose fixed replies skip the model entirely. With both off, fee questions and those guard-answered questions all go to the model, which is the likely reason for the slower answers and the tail at 8.4 seconds, though I did not time that split. Only about seven of the 40 questions are fee calculations, so the P95 rests on very few answers.

What the results show

Each pipeline component helps on a test it was not tuned on. Cutting snippets to 1,000 characters costs 26.1 points on unseen rulings. Turning the calculator off drops fee questions from 100% to 20%. Removing case scoping costs 4.7 points overall, the smallest effect.

Fine-tuning shows no detectable benefit at this sample size, and every interval includes zero.

What they do not show

That fine-tuning is useless. The unseen rulings come from only 14 cases, so a gain of a few points could hide in the intervals.

Statute questions cannot be truly unseen, and the judge is itself a model. I re-judged 30 randomly drawn answers by hand (seed 20261017) and agreed with the judge on all 30. Only 13 of those were scored by the model judge, the other 17 being deterministic checks on fee figures and refusals, so the real evidence is 13 for 13, which still allows a disagreement rate of about 20% or less at 95% confidence. One jurisdiction, one 7B model, one adapter of each kind.

The fee router and its guard also misfire: on 6 of the 236 questions (about 2.5%) they replied with a fixed fee message to a question that was not a fee calculation, so the full pipeline's accuracy is somewhat understated.
